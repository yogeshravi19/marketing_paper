## PART B: FULL LITERATURE REVIEW IN CLEAR, SIMPLE ENGLISH (For Your Research Paper)

### 2. Literature Review

#### 2.1 Small Business Digital Marketing and the "Likes vs. Sales" Problem
Small businesses today rely heavily on digital channels—such as Instagram, Facebook, Google Search, and WhatsApp—to attract new customers on a small budget (Ahmad et al., 2019; Roosdhani, Farida & Indriani, 2023; De, 2025). However, research shows that simply being active on social media does not automatically bring sales. In a study of **144 small and medium enterprises (SMEs)** in the UAE, Ahmad, Abu Bakar & Ahmad (2019) found that adopting social media had **no statistically significant effect on business performance (β = 0.100, p = 0.11, R² = 0.01)**. Their regression results showed that small businesses joined social media mainly due to **"bandwagon pressure" (β = 0.204, p = 0.018)**—copying competitors out of fear of missing out, without knowing how to turn followers into paying customers.

Explaining how small businesses can move *"From Likes to Sales,"* Roosdhani, Farida & Indriani (2023) surveyed **265 food and beverage SMEs** in Indonesia using Structural Equation Modeling (CB-SEM in AMOS). They proved that a **social media marketing strategy has no direct effect on performance by itself (β = 0.102, p = 0.412)**; instead, it only improves performance when it successfully builds **brand exposure (β = 0.827, p < 0.001)** and **active customer engagement (β = 0.823, p < 0.001)**, such as customers commenting, asking questions, and contacting the business. Yet, despite how critical new customer acquisition is for small business survival, Azhar & Juidette’s (2025) PRISMA systematic review of **263 studies (2015–2024)** revealed that **only 12 studies worldwide** focus on how digital marketing drives **customer acquisition** (as opposed to brand awareness or keeping old customers), with only one qualitative study on SMEs. Furthermore, through in-depth interviews with **14 Indian small business owners**, De (2025) showed why standard Western e-commerce models do not fit Indian small businesses: small sellers use Instagram and Facebook reels for initial discovery, but rely on **direct conversational touchpoints—specifically WhatsApp Business catalogs, one-on-one chat, status updates, and UPI payment links—to build personal trust and close the first sale**. Because Ahmad et al. (2019), Roosdhani et al. (2023), and De (2025) only studied **business owners**, the literature still lacks customer-side data showing which digital touchpoints actually convince a buyer to place their first order.

#### 2.2 Digital Touchpoint Types and the Multi-Stage Customer Journey
When a customer considers buying from a business for the first time, they rarely rely on a single ad or post; instead, they move through multiple digital touchpoints across discovery, evaluation, and purchase stages (Baxendale et al., 2015; Herhausen et al., 2019; Moliner & Tortosa-Edo, 2024). In a study of **2,348 consumers** evaluating nine digital touchpoints, Hallikainen, Alamäki & Laukkanen (2019) grouped digital touchpoints into three clear types: **Functional Touchpoints** (search engines, brand websites, email, and live chat), **Social Touchpoints** (social networks, Instagram photo communities, and YouTube video communities), and **Community Touchpoints** (blogs and forums). Across every consumer segment, **functional touchpoints—especially search engines (M = 3.98) and brand websites (M = 3.86)—were rated much more important for making an actual purchase than social media (M = 1.98–2.46)**, which mainly sparked initial interest.

Looking across the broader shopping journey, Herhausen et al. (2019) tracked **17 pre-purchase and purchase touchpoints** (including websites, search engines, online reviews, price comparison sites, social media, email, mobile apps, stores, and word-of-mouth) across **5,092 customer journeys** (2,443 in Study 1 and 2,649 in Study 2), showing that customers who use mobile and social media touchpoints quickly switch to competitors if their journey experience is unsatisfactory. Similarly, surveying **205 multi-channel shoppers** (filtered from 1,458 responses) using CB-SEM, Moliner & Tortosa-Edo (2024) proved that well-connected, consistent touchpoints across the journey (**Omnichannel Journey Design**) strongly improve **Online Customer Experience (β = 0.385)** and **Customer e-Satisfaction (β = 0.657)**. Importantly, in a real-time mobile tracking study of **13,962 customer journeys**, Baxendale, Macdonald & Wilson (2015) proved that **touchpoint positivity (`AvgPos`—how helpful and positive the interaction felt)** explains far more about customer decisions than simply **how many times (`Freq`) the customer saw the touchpoint**. Finally, across **16 experiments** (including **10,470 consumers** in Study 1 and a live field test on Amazon in Study 2), Bleier, Harmeling & Palmatier (2019) proved a critical rule for small businesses: **when a brand is unfamiliar or less trusted, customers rely much more heavily on social presence (such as conversational tone and customer star ratings) and clear product information to feel safe enough to buy**. However, all of these journey studies focused on brand consideration, satisfaction, or repeat loyalty at large retailers rather than first-time customer acquisition at small businesses.

#### 2.3 Inside the Customer’s Mind: Engagement, Brand Trust, Online Reviews (eWOM), and Perceived Value
Digital touchpoints do not turn a visitor into a buyer automatically; they work by changing how the customer thinks and feels. Hollebeek & Macky (2019) developed a clear three-step psychological framework showing how digital content and touchpoints work: first, helpful and enjoyable digital interactions create **Customer Engagement** (thinking about, feeling connected to, and interacting with the brand); second, that engagement builds **Brand Trust** (believing the seller is honest, reliable, and cares about the customer); and third, trust leads to **Perceived Value** (believing the product is worth the money and effort).

Empirical studies confirm that different digital touchpoints play very different roles in building Engagement versus Trust and Value. Surveying **625 Indian consumers** who shop via WhatsApp, Instagram, Facebook, and YouTube, George, Joseph, Abraham & Joseph (2023) found that **Likes and Shares** strongly boost **Brand Engagement (β = 0.47, p < 0.001)** but have the weakest effect on **Brand Trust (β = 0.25)**. In contrast, **one-on-one chat interaction (β = 0.33 on Trust; β = 0.48 on Engagement)** and **Online Reviews and Ratings (β = 0.32 on Trust)** are what actually build **Brand Trust (R² = 0.38)**. This explains why social media likes alone fail to convert: likes create surface engagement, while reviews and direct chat build the trust needed to buy. Comparing peer recommendations on **Social Media** versus **Shopping Website Reviews** across **384 UK shoppers** and **10 interviews**, Erkan & Evans (2018) proved that **shopping website reviews strongly drive Online Purchase Intention through Information Quality (β = 0.358), Credibility (β = 0.307), Usefulness (β = 0.412), and Adoption (β = 0.430, p < 0.001), whereas social media posts had near-zero direct impact on purchase intention (β = -0.124 to 0.000)**. Confirming this across **69 studies** (up to **29,593 consumers**), Ismagilova et al.’s (2020) meta-analysis proved that **the mere number (volume) of online comments is NOT statistically significant (r = 0.245, p = 0.068)**; instead, **Trust in the Message (r = 0.506)**, **Usefulness (r = 0.496)**, and **Argument Quality (r = 0.449)** are what drive buying intention.

#### 2.4 Bridging the Gap Between "Purchase Intention" and "Actual First Purchase"
A major weakness in most digital marketing surveys—including Erkan & Evans (2018), Ismagilova et al. (2020), and George et al. (2023)—is that they stop at **Purchase Intention** (asking people if they *plan* to buy) instead of measuring **Actual Purchase** (whether they *actually paid and bought*). Combining **189 findings from 68 social commerce studies** using Meta-Analytic SEM (MASEM), Dwivedi et al. (2023) proved that **Purchase Intention does not equal Actual Buying**: while model variables explained **48.5% of Purchase Intention (R² = 0.485)**, the link from **Purchase Intention to Actual Buying Behavior was weak (β = 0.28, p < 0.01)**, explaining **29.2% of actual behavior (R² = 0.292)**. Even more importantly, Dwivedi et al. (2023) discovered that **Trust (β = 0.33, p < 0.01)** and **Emotional Support (β = 0.16, p < 0.01)** have **direct effects on Actual Buying Behavior**, proving that when buying from an unfamiliar seller, a customer needs strong trust at the final moment of payment to complete the order.

To test both psychology and real buying in one study, Kim, Ferrin & Rao (2008) created a two-stage research design across **468 online shoppers**—comparing **261 actual buyers (Y = 1)** against **207 non-buyers (Y = 0)** who visited and considered buying but did not purchase. They used **PLS-SEM** for the psychological variables (`Information Quality`, `Reputation`, `Trust`, `Perceived Risk`, `Perceived Benefit/Value`, and `Purchase Intention`, R² = 0.339) and **Binary Logistic Regression** to predict **Actual Purchase (Y = 1 vs. Y = 0, R² = 0.301, Wald = 67.32, p < 0.001)**. Their method provides the exact blueprint for our study to compare consumers who actually completed their first purchase from a small business (Y = 1) against those who checked the business’s digital touchpoints but decided not to buy (Y = 0).

#### 2.5 Multi-Touch Marketing Attribution and Explainable Conversion Analytics
Finally, marketing analytics studies examine how to divide conversion credit fairly when a customer uses several digital touchpoints before buying. Reviewing **62 marketing attribution studies**, Buhalis & Volchek (2021) organized attribution methods into five groups—including simple rules (first-click, last-click), statistical regressions (Binary Logistic Regression), Markov models, and machine learning / game-theory methods (**Shapley Values / SHAP**)—and proved that giving 100% of the credit to the "last click" is misleading because earlier touchpoints (like social media discovery and reviews) set up the sale. Using real multi-channel conversion data from JD.com, Du, Zhong, Nair, Cui & Shou (2019) showed how **Binary Logistic Regression**, **Machine Learning**, and **Shapley Value (SHAP) attribution** calculate the exact percentage contribution of each digital touchpoint toward a binary purchase decision (Y = 1 vs. Y = 0).

However, Buhalis & Volchek (2021, pp. 31–39) pointed out a major blind spot in all current attribution models: **big-data attribution algorithms only count clicks from company tracking servers (which small businesses do not have when customers jump from Instagram Reels to Google Reviews to WhatsApp Chat), and they completely ignore the customer's psychology (`Engagement → Trust → Perceived Value`) that explains *why* those touchpoints caused the sale**.

#### 2.6 Summary of the Research Gap and Our Study’s Contribution
Putting all 17 Core Papers together reveals three clear gaps that our study solves:
1. **Small-Business Customer Gap:** Only 12 studies globally examine digital marketing for customer acquisition (Azhar & Juidette, 2025), and past small-business studies only surveyed shop owners (Ahmad et al., 2019; Roosdhani et al., 2023; De, 2025) instead of asking customers which touchpoints actually convinced them to buy for the first time.
2. **Touchpoint & Real Conversion Gap:** Past consumer studies either stopped at **Purchase Intention** instead of **Actual First Purchase** (ignoring the β = 0.28 intention–behavior gap proved by Dwivedi et al., 2023) or studied big-brand websites while leaving out **Direct Conversational Touchpoints (WhatsApp Business / Instagram DMs)** that are vital for Indian small businesses (De, 2025; George et al., 2023).
3. **Analytics + Psychology Gap:** Multi-touch attribution models count clicks without customer psychology (Buhalis & Volchek, 2021; Du et al., 2019), while psychology models rarely rank touchpoints on actual binary conversion (Y = 1 buyer vs. Y = 0 non-buyer; Kim, Ferrin & Rao, 2008).

**How Our Study Solves This Gap:** We combine **PLS-SEM** (to test how **Social Media**, **Search/Website**, **Online Reviews**, and **WhatsApp/DM Chat** build **Engagement → Trust & Perceived Value → Purchase Intention**) with **Binary Conversion & Shapley Value (SHAP) Attribution** (comparing **Actual First-Time Buyers [Y = 1]** vs. **Non-Buyers [Y = 0]**) to identify the exact digital touchpoints that drive customer acquisition in small businesses.
